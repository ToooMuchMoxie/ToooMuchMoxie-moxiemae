Moxie Mae’s Take #015 — Dossier

The Blueprint Was Breakfast

At almost 90, Black Panther Party co-founder Bobby Seale told ABC7 how it actually worked. Mae goes to the organizer himself — not the movie version.

The Claim

Sixty years after two Merritt College students founded the Black Panther Party on Oct 15, 1966 in Oakland, Seale's own account is plain: it started with a named harm — discrimination — and a decision to organize. What kept the Party alive wasn't the rhetoric. It was the programs.

The Receipts

Receipt #1 — They wrote it down

Seale and Huey Newton produced a Ten-Point Program: full employment, decent housing fit for human beings, education teaching true history, an immediate end to police brutality — closing on “land, bread, housing, education, clothing, justice, and peace.” Asked what it demanded, the answer is four words deep: jobs, housing, education, and protection from the police.

Source: ABC7 interview with Bobby Seale (published Oct 18, 2026); Ten-Point Program (Take #015 file)

Receipt #2 — Organize — his verb

On the killing of young Black men by police, Seale's own sentence: “Get to organize people.” The order of operations in his memory: discrimination, then organize. ABC7 counted the cost by 1970 — ten police officers and eleven Panthers dead — and Mae doesn't romanticize it.

Source: ABC7 interview, video receipt VR2 in the Take (Take #015 script, Seg 3)

Receipt #3 — The programs

The Free Breakfast for Children Program began January 1969 at St. Augustine's Church in Oakland — kids fed before school, no forms, no fee. By the end of 1969 the breakfasts alone served 20,000 children in cities across the country; the survival programs passed 65 in all. Around 70% of the Party's membership was women.

Source: Take #015 script, Seg 4 (ABC7 interview and Party record)

Receipt #4 — The line the Take is built on

Leslie Johnson-Seale — who joined as a 20-year-old breakfast-program volunteer and has been married to the chairman 54 years — verbatim: “Beyond all the rhetoric — those programs is what kept the party alive.”

Source: ABC7 interview, video receipt VR3 in the Take (Take #015 script, Seg 5)

Mae’s Verdict“Service is what people remember when the cameras bend the corner.” A demand written down can be organized around. A demand that stays a feeling just gets felt — the Panthers put theirs on paper before they put on the berets.
